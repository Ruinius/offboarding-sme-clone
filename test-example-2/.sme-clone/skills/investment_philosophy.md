# SKILL: Investment Philosophy & Stock Selection

> Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway Chairman's Letters, 1977–2024.
> Evidence base: 48 annual letters spanning four decades of investment decisions.

---

## Triggers

Activate this skill when someone asks:
- How to evaluate a stock or equity investment
- What makes a business worth owning
- When to buy, hold, or sell a position
- How to think about market prices vs. business value
- Whether to invest in a particular sector or company type
- How to think about diversification, concentration, or position sizing

---

## Context

Warren Buffett's investment philosophy is grounded in a simple principle: **buying equity in a business is the same act as buying the business itself, just at a smaller scale.** The analytical framework, the patience required, and the criteria for quality are identical whether you are buying 100% or 0.01% of a company.

The philosophy was shaped primarily by two mentors:
- **Ben Graham**: Emphasis on margin of safety, price vs. value distinction, and the "Mr. Market" metaphor for market irrationality.
- **Charlie Munger**: Shift from purely quantitative "cigar butt" investing to paying fair prices for *wonderful businesses* rather than cheap prices for mediocre ones.

## Decision Logic

### Step 1 — Pass the "Understanding" Filter
> "We want the business to be one that we can understand." (1977)

- Can you explain the business model in two sentences?
- Do you understand *why* customers pay for this product and what would make them stop?
- If no: **stop here**. Do not proceed.

### Step 2 — Assess Long-Term Economics
> "With favorable long-term prospects." (1977)

Ask:
- Is the business in an industry where the structural tailwinds (not management heroism) favor the owner?
- Does the company earn returns on equity capital that consistently exceed its cost of capital?
- Is there a **durable competitive advantage** (moat)?
  - Cost advantage (GEICO's direct-to-consumer model)
  - Brand/switching costs (Coca-Cola, See's Candies)
  - Network effects or regulatory protection (BNSF railroads)
- Can the business sustain pricing power over time independent of general inflation?

Disqualifiers:
- "Commodity" businesses where price is the only differentiator
- Businesses requiring constant heavy reinvestment just to maintain market position
- Capital-intensive businesses with low returns on incremental capital

### Step 3 — Evaluate the Management
> "Operated by honest and competent people." (1977)

- Does management allocate capital rationally (not empire-building)?
- Do they communicate transparently, including about mistakes?
- Are they owner-oriented rather than manager-oriented? (Do they behave the same way whether or not you are watching?)
- Do they have a demonstrated long-term track record, not just a recent good year?

Buffett specifically **does not** require:
- Prestigious educational credentials
- A charismatic personality
- Frequent public appearances

### Step 4 — Price Discipline
> "Available at a very attractive price." (1977)

- What is the intrinsic value? *(Present value of owner earnings over the long term.)*
- Is the current market price meaningfully below intrinsic value?
- **Wonderful company at a fair price > Fair company at a wonderful price** (Munger's influence from 1981 onward)
- A margin of safety is required, but the size of margin scales with the certainty of the underlying earnings estimate.

Buffett's stance on price waiting:
> "Doing nothing is the most difficult task of all." (1984)
> "Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are usually greeted by yawns." (2008)

### Step 5 — Hold Decision Logic
Once owned:
- **Default: hold forever.** The decision to sell is harder than the decision to buy.
- Sell when: (a) the original thesis has been invalidated by business deterioration, (b) a far better opportunity exists and capital reallocation creates clear per-share value, or (c) price is so extreme above intrinsic value that holding is irrational.
- **Never sell because of macro predictions** (interest rates, elections, recessions, market levels).
- Price declines in positions you own with high conviction are opportunities to buy more, not reasons to sell.

## Key Principles (Distilled)

| Principle | Source Year |
| :--- | :---: |
| Buy businesses, not stocks | 1977 |
| Price is what you pay; value is what you get (Graham) | 2008 |
| Wonderful company at a fair price beats fair company at wonderful price | 1981+ |
| The stock market is a wealth transfer from the impatient to the patient | 1991 |
| "Look-through earnings" matter more than reported earnings | 1991 |
| Ignore short-term market forecasts entirely | 1977, 1987, 2008 |
| Concentration, not diversification, for high-conviction ideas | Multiple |
| Inactivity is often the highest-value action | 1984, 2008 |

---

## What Buffett Explicitly Avoids

- Businesses he cannot understand (technology with unpredictable economics, pre-1999)
- Hostile takeovers (on principle, not just tactically)
- IPOs as a class (the seller knows more than the buyer)
- Cyclical businesses at peak earnings with peak multiples
- Financial leverage at the Berkshire parent level
- EBITDA as a valuation metric (*"a flawed favorite of Wall Street"*, 2015)
- Back-tested models or academic formulas (2008)
