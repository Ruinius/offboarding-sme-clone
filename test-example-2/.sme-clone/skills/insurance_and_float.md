# SKILL: Insurance, Float, and Capital Structure

> Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway Chairman's Letters, 1977–2024.

---

## Triggers

Activate this skill when someone asks:
- How insurance creates investment capital ("float")
- What makes a P/C insurer excellent vs. mediocre
- How to evaluate insurance underwriting quality
- Why Berkshire maintains such conservative capital levels
- How to think about leverage in financial businesses
- What the "combined ratio" means and how to interpret it
- Why Berkshire never uses debt aggressively

---

## Context

Insurance is Berkshire's **core business** and has been since 1967. The central insight that makes insurance special is **float**: the time delay between receiving premiums upfront and paying claims later. This pool of investable capital — belonging to policyholders but temporarily held by the insurer — is the engine that funded Berkshire's entire investment operation.

As of 2024, Berkshire's float has grown to **$171 billion**.

> "Float — which we generate in exceptional amounts — is the total of loss reserves, loss adjustment expense reserves, and unearned premium reserves minus agents balances, prepaid acquisition costs, and deferred charges." (1991)

The cost of float is the underwriting profit or loss. If the insurer breaks even or profits on underwriting (combined ratio ≤ ~100), the float is *free* or even *negatively priced* — better than borrowing money.

## Decision Logic

### Evaluating Float Quality

The only metric that matters for float quality:

**Cost of float = Underwriting loss / Average float**

Interpretation:
- If cost of float < risk-free rate: exceptional performance
- If cost of float ≈ risk-free rate: acceptable
- If cost of float > risk-free rate: float is a liability, not an asset

Buffett tracked this metric from 1967 through 2024. Berkshire's cost of float was **below the U.S. government's borrowing rate in 20 of the first 25 years** of insurance operations (as of 1991 letter).

### The Combined Ratio

**Combined ratio = (Losses incurred + Expenses) / Premium revenue**

- Below 100: underwriting profit (float costs nothing)
- Above 100: underwriting loss (float has a cost)
- The combined ratio alone is misleading without the float volume: a 105 combined ratio with massive float may be better economics than a 98 combined ratio with tiny float

> "A combined ratio in the 107–111 range typically produces an overall break-even result, exclusive of earnings on the funds provided by shareholders." (1991)

### What Makes an Insurance Operation Excellent

1. **Underwriting discipline**: Willingness to shrink when prices are inadequate; not driven by premium volume targets
2. **Reserving conservatism**: Loss reserves that slightly overstate liabilities (surprises are pleasant, not terrible)
3. **Cost culture**: Low expense ratios enable profitability at lower prices
4. **Float growth**: Growing volume at acceptable underwriting losses = growing investment capital at no cost
5. **Financial strength**: Ability to pay claims in catastrophic scenarios (when other insurers cannot)

> "No private insurer has the willingness to take on the amount of risk that Berkshire can provide... We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide." (2024)

### Insurance Reserving Risks

Loss reserve errors are the primary financial reporting risk in P/C insurance:
- Long-tail lines (medical malpractice, product liability, asbestos) may have losses that don't emerge for decades
- Optimistic or dishonest reserving produces fictitious profits for years before the truth emerges
- Pattern: *"The combination of an optimist or a crook in the CEO chair with long-tail insurance lines is the most dangerous configuration in finance."* (2024, paraphrased from direct text)

Berkshire's response: Ajit Jain's reinsurance division **never books optimistic reserves**. Mike Goldberg's instruction remains apt: *"We want our underwriters to daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed."* (2024)

### Catastrophe (Super-Cat) Insurance

Berkshire is one of the world's largest writers of catastrophe reinsurance — coverage that protects other insurers from severe losses.

Key characteristics:
- Results are extremely volatile year-to-year
- Premiums expected to cover ~90% of losses over the long run
- In any given year: could be massive profit (no catastrophes) or massive loss (multiple major events)
- GAAP accounting does not allow catastrophe reserves in loss-free years, making annual results misleading

Berkshire's advantage: **financial strength allows it to make this commitment credibly**. When a "big one" comes, many reinsurers who wrote policies cannot write checks. Berkshire can.

### Capital Structure Philosophy

Berkshire maintains an **absolute commitment to financial fortress-level strength**:
- Minimum $20B in cash and liquid instruments at all times (stated policy, 2017 onward)
- No meaningful debt at the parent company level
- No reliance on external capital markets for operations
- Willing to forego returns from leverage that other companies would accept

Rationale (the "one in fifty years" principle):
- Every fifty years or so, a crisis occurs in which counterparties, credit lines, and normal market operations freeze simultaneously
- Companies dependent on external capital in that moment face extinction or dilutive rescue
- Berkshire's goal: **never be in a position to need the kindness of strangers** (2008)

> "When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits." (2008)

### Derivatives Risk Framework

Buffett's position is categorical: **derivatives are dangerous instruments that create systemic risk and should be avoided except when economics are clearly compelling and Berkshire holds the cash upfront**.

Why dangerous:
1. Counterparty risk accumulates over years or decades
2. Books are impossible to audit meaningfully
3. Leverage and risk are hidden from management, auditors, and regulators
4. Mark-to-model valuations allow fictitious earnings
5. When counterparties fail simultaneously (as in 2008), the losses are correlated and severe

Berkshire's exception: Some derivative positions where Berkshire *receives* the premium upfront (no counterparty credit risk) and believes prices are dramatically mispriced. Even then: managed personally by Buffett with full board awareness.

> "Derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal." (2002)
