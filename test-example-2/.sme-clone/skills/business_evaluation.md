# SKILL: Business Evaluation & Acquisitions

> Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway Chairman's Letters, 1977–2024.

---

## Triggers

Activate this skill when someone asks:
- How to evaluate whether to acquire a company
- What criteria Berkshire uses to buy businesses
- What price is appropriate for an acquisition
- How to think about goodwill, tangible assets, and intrinsic value in M&A
- Why some acquisitions destroy value and others create it
- How to structure a deal with a founder-owner

---

## Context

Buffett distinguishes two types of equity ownership:
1. **Partial ownership** via publicly traded shares (marketable securities)
2. **100% ownership** via negotiated acquisitions

The evaluation criteria are nearly identical, but the constraints differ. With public stocks, you can exit if you're wrong. With controlled companies, *you almost never sell*, which raises the bar for entry.

> "In reality, Berkshire almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems." (2024)

## Decision Logic

### The Six Acquisition Criteria (Stable Since 1991)

> Stated publicly and repeatedly. Any target must satisfy **all six**:

1. **Large purchases** — at least $10M of after-tax earnings (in 1991 terms; scale accordingly)
2. **Demonstrated consistent earning power** — future projections are of little interest; turnarounds are not sought
3. **Good returns on equity while employing little or no debt**
4. **Management in place** — Berkshire cannot supply it
5. **Simple businesses** — "if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"
6. **An offering price** — preliminary talks without price waste everyone's time

> "We will not engage in unfriendly takeovers." (1991)

### Evaluating Business Quality

The key question: **Is this a "franchise" or a "business"?** (1991, 1986)

**Franchise characteristics**:
- Product or service that is *needed or desired*
- Customers perceive *no close substitute*
- Not subject to price regulation
- Can sustain aggressive pricing and earn high returns on capital
- Can survive poor management (though not thrive)

**Commodity business characteristics**:
- Competing primarily on price
- Customers defect immediately for cheaper alternatives
- Returns on capital revert to the cost of capital over time
- Requires excellent management just to maintain mediocre results

**Implication**: A franchise is worth a premium over book value. A commodity business is worth roughly book value. Do not pay franchise prices for commodity businesses — the most common M&A mistake.

### The Goodwill Question

Buffett distinguishes:
- **Accounting goodwill**: The excess paid over book value, amortized under GAAP (before 2001)
- **Economic goodwill**: The true earning power above what a capital-equivalent generic business would earn

> "Businesses logically are worth far more than net tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings on such assets considerably in excess of market rates of return." (1983)

For a franchise, **economic goodwill does not depreciate** — it may appreciate. Amortizing it against earnings (as GAAP required pre-2001) systematically understated Berkshire's true earnings.

### What to Pay

Framework (in plain language):
1. Estimate **owner earnings** = Net income + Depreciation − Maintenance capex ± working capital changes
2. Apply a **capitalization rate** appropriate for a business of this quality and growth (not a spreadsheet formula — a judgment)
3. Require a **margin of safety** inversely proportional to your certainty
4. If the seller's price exceeds your estimate of intrinsic value: **walk away, however attractive the story**

> "We'd rather buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." (Munger, as cited repeatedly)

The most dangerous acquisition error: **overpaying during euphoric markets**.

### The Founder-Seller Dynamic

Buffett's preferred acquisition type: founder-operators who want to:
- Monetize part of their wealth for themselves or their family
- Continue running the company as before
- Find a permanent, non-meddling owner for their "baby"

> "What we must do is provide a concert hall in which business artists of this class will wish to perform." (1991)

Key practices:
- No requirement for audited financials before signing (see NFM example: gave Mrs. B a $55M check on her word)
- No bureaucratic valuation committee
- Decision made quickly: *"We can promise a very fast answer — customarily within five minutes — as to whether we're interested."* (1991)
- Management autonomy post-acquisition: Berkshire does not relocate, rename, restructure, or interfere

### Post-Acquisition Management Model

Berkshire's operating philosophy:
- **Complete operational autonomy** for subsidiary managers
- No required budgets submitted to Omaha
- No strategic planning sessions with corporate
- Capital allocated by Berkshire parent, not subsidiary managers
- Subsidiary cash flows up to the parent (where Buffett allocates them)
- Managers compensated based on **returns on capital employed** in their specific business, not Berkshire's overall stock price

> "My job is merely to treat them right and to allocate the capital they generate." (1991)

## Common Mistakes in M&A (Observed in Others)

| Mistake | Consequence |
| :--- | :--- |
| Paying for "synergies" that never materialize | Permanent capital destruction |
| Acquiring to deploy excess cash rather than from strategic logic | Average or worse returns |
| Using overvalued stock as currency | Effectively diluting existing owners |
| LBO/private equity exit mentality ("merchandise") | Misaligned with sellers who care about legacy |
| Replacing excellent founder-managers post-acquisition | Losing the primary value driver |
| Treating acquisition as turnaround opportunity | "Turnarounds seldom turn" (recurring Buffett warning) |
