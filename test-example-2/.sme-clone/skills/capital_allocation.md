# SKILL: Capital Allocation & Corporate Finance

> Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway Chairman's Letters, 1977–2024.

---

## Triggers

Activate this skill when someone asks:
- Whether a company should pay dividends, buy back stock, or reinvest
- How to evaluate a share buyback
- What "return on equity" vs. "earnings per share" growth means
- How to think about incentive compensation and executive pay
- Why Berkshire doesn't pay dividends
- How to evaluate a company's use of retained earnings
- What "owner earnings" are and why they matter

---

## Context

Capital allocation is the most important skill a business manager can have, and the most underappreciated. Buffett argues that:

> "The heads of many companies are not skilled in capital allocation. Their inadequacy is not surprising. Most bosses rise to the top because they have excelled in an area such as marketing, production, engineering, administration or, sometimes, institutional politics. Once they become CEOs, they face new responsibilities." (cited from various letters)

The consequence: most companies deploy retained earnings suboptimally because the decision-maker was promoted for a different skill set.

## Decision Logic

### The Capital Allocation Hierarchy

When a business generates excess cash, there are exactly five things that can be done with it (in order of Buffett's preference given the right conditions):

1. **Reinvest in the existing business at above-average returns** — the best outcome, when opportunities exist
2. **Make acquisitions at sensible prices** — second best, when businesses meeting Berkshire's criteria are available
3. **Repurchase shares at prices below intrinsic value** — creates per-share value for remaining shareholders
4. **Pay a dividend** — acceptable but only if options 1–3 are unavailable at reasonable returns; implies management has no better use for the capital
5. **Hold as cash** — appropriate temporarily while waiting for options 1–3

> "Appropriate action: The test of intelligent capital allocation is that each dollar retained must create at least one dollar of long-term market value." (paraphrased from 1984)

### Share Repurchases

Buffett's position on buybacks is nuanced and firm:

**When repurchases are good**: When shares trade significantly below intrinsic value, repurchasing is the highest-return use of capital because you are buying a business you know intimately at a discount. Every remaining share becomes worth more.

> "When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable financial positions find their shares selling far below intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases." (1984)

**When repurchases are bad** (or neutral):
- When done at prices *above* intrinsic value (transfers wealth from continuing shareholders to sellers)
- When done to offset dilution from excessive stock option programs (running in place)
- When done to "manage" EPS numbers rather than because value creation is genuine
- "Greenmail" repurchases: buying out a hostile party at a premium at the expense of other shareholders. *"A practice we find odious and repugnant."* (1984)

**Buffett's test**: If you wouldn't buy the whole company at today's price, you shouldn't buy shares at today's price.

### Dividends

Buffett's position on dividends (consistently held across all 48 letters):

Berkshire has paid exactly **one cash dividend** in 60 years — $101,755 on January 3, 1967, or 10¢ per share.

> "I can't remember why I suggested this action to Berkshire's board of directors. Now it seems like a bad dream." (2024)

The rationale for not paying dividends:
- Berkshire can consistently deploy retained earnings at returns exceeding what shareholders could earn by receiving the dividend and reinvesting it themselves
- A dollar retained by Berkshire should create more than a dollar of market value for shareholders
- The "Aesop test": is there enough certainty that the future cash flows justify forgoing current income? At Berkshire, yes.

**When dividends make sense for other companies**: When the company cannot deploy retained earnings at returns above its cost of capital. Then it should return capital to shareholders (either dividends or buybacks) rather than making substandard reinvestments.

### Earnings Metrics — What to Use and What to Avoid

| Metric | Buffett's View |
| :--- | :--- |
| **EPS (earnings per share)** | Useful only alongside capital used. A 10% EPS gain funded by a 20% equity increase is mediocre, not impressive. |
| **Return on equity (ROE)** | The primary metric for management performance: operating earnings / beginning equity capital |
| **Owner earnings** | The best measure of true cash generation: net income + depreciation − maintenance capex ± working capital changes |
| **GAAP net income** | Adequate for most purposes but distorted by amortization of goodwill (pre-2001), unrealized gains/losses, and purchase accounting adjustments |
| **EBITDA** | *"A flawed favorite of Wall Street."* Misleads by ignoring the real capital cost of depreciation. Reject it. |
| **Operating earnings (Berkshire's definition)** | Excludes realized and unrealized capital gains/losses. The most stable signal of underlying business health. |

### Incentive Compensation Philosophy

The test: **Does the compensation system create the right incentives, or does it reward managers for outcomes they did not cause?**

Buffett's principles:
- Pay for performance on the specific capital employed in the manager's own business, not for Berkshire's overall results or stock price (managers have no control over those)
- Avoid options that give upside with no downside — they are one-sided bets at shareholders' expense
- H.H. Brown's compensation model (cited as ideal in 1991): annual salary of $7,800 plus a designated percentage of profits *above* a return threshold on capital employed — managers "truly stand in the shoes of owners"
- If you lose money, managers who are aligned should lose money too

> "Most managers talk the talk but don't walk the walk, choosing instead to employ compensation systems that are long on carrots but short on sticks." (1991)

## Key Principles (Distilled)

| Principle | Source Year |
| :--- | :--- |
| Return on equity, not EPS growth, measures management quality | 1977 |
| Every retained dollar must create at least one dollar of value | 1984 |
| Repurchases good below intrinsic value; destructive above it | 1984 |
| Greenmail is odious; genuine buybacks are excellent | 1984 |
| Dividends signal inability to deploy capital productively | 1984 onward |
| Owner earnings > GAAP earnings as cash-flow measure | 1981 |
| EBITDA is a misleading and dangerous metric | 2015 |
| Compensation should align with owner economics | 1993 |
